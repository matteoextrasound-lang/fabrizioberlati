Record skipping, crackling or humming What to check, and in what order, when the record skips, makes noise or the turntable hums

The cause is almost always simple and can be found in a few minutes. The trick is to start from the symptom and check things in the right order, from most to least likely.

By Fabrizio Berlati · Artisan workshop in Cesena

First: one record or all of them?

The first question is simple. If the problem happens on one record only, the cause is almost certainly that record: dirty, scratched or warped. If it happens on every record, the cause is in the turntable or the system.

For hum there's one more test: play another source, such as your phone or CD player, through the same amplifier. If that hums too, the problem isn't the turntable but the amplifier or the speakers.

The record skips.

The most common causes are two tonearm settings. You can check them in a few minutes.

  • Tracking force. Every cartridge has a value specified by its maker, in grams. If the stylus is too light it jumps out of the groove; if it's too heavy it wears out both record and stylus. You set it with the tonearm's counterweight and check it with a stylus force gauge.
  • Anti-skate. While the record spins, the tonearm is pulled towards the centre. Anti-skate compensates with a small outward push, and it's usually set to the same value as the tracking force.
  • The record. If only one record skips, and always at the same spot, the problem is that record, not the turntable: usually there's dirt in the groove, sometimes a scratch. Clean it and try again.
  • A dirty stylus. A ball of fluff on the stylus makes it slide out of the groove. Further on in this guide you'll find how to clean it.

If the record skips when you walk near the furniture or turn the volume up, the cause is vibration: you'll find everything in the guide on how to isolate a turntable from vibration.

Crackles, ticks and hiss.

It's almost always dust in the groove, held there by static electricity. The record builds up a charge when you slide it out of its sleeve or clean it, and the charge pulls dust out of the air. It happens with new records too: they need brushing as well.

  • A carbon-fibre brush before every play. Rest it on the spinning record for a couple of turns, then slowly sweep it towards the edge.
  • Anti-static inner sleeves. The record builds up less static when you slide it out. I cover this in the guide on how to store vinyl records.
  • A proper wash for very dirty records. A proper cleaning fluid and a machine that vacuums the dirt out of the groove. Extrasound also offers a record cleaning service with a Pro-Ject machine.
  • No household products. Washing-up liquid, glass cleaner and kitchen paper can damage vinyl: use only products made for records.

Part of the noise comes from micro-scratches, tiny marks a record picks up when you slide it out of its sleeve or clean it badly. They can't be removed, but you can avoid them by handling records carefully.

The stylus: clean and not worn.

The stylus collects dust too. Clean it with its brush, always from back to front, in the direction the record moves, never sideways.

Styli wear out. If the noise persists even on clean records and the sound has become muddy or distorted, have it checked and replace it if needed: a worn stylus also damages records.

It hums.

A low, constant hum that you hear even with no record playing is almost always a grounding problem.

  • The ground wire. Alongside the signal cables, a thin wire with a spade connector comes out of the turntable. It should be screwed to the GND terminal on the amplifier or phono preamp. In rare cases the hum drops when it's disconnected: if connecting it doesn't help, try that too.
  • The cartridge wires. The four thin wires that connect the cartridge to the tonearm must be firmly in place, and the cartridge must be securely fixed to the arm.
  • Away from the amplifier. The amplifier's transformer and nearby power supplies can make the cartridge hum. Never put the turntable on top of the amplifier: I cover this in the guide on where to put your amplifier.
  • The cable. Try another cable: a faulty cable can hum too.

A faint hum that you only hear at very high volume with no record playing is normal.

It sounds distorted, or quiet and thin.

The cartridge's signal is extremely weak: before it reaches the amplifier it has to be amplified and equalised by a phono preamp. It can be inside the amplifier (the PHONO input), inside the turntable or in a separate small box. The rule is that you need exactly one.

  • Distorted. The turntable's built-in preamp is on and it's connected to the PHONO input: the signal is amplified twice. Set the turntable's switch to PHONO, or connect it to a regular input.
  • Quiet and thin. The turntable has no preamp, or it's switched off, and it's connected to a regular input (LINE, AUX) or to active speakers. Switch on the turntable's preamp by setting the switch to LINE, or use the PHONO input or an external preamp.

Frequently asked questions.

Why does my record skip?

Most often because the tracking force or anti-skate is set wrong, or because the record is dirty or scratched. If it skips when you walk near the furniture, vibration is the cause.

Why does a new record crackle?

New records also build up static and attract dust. A pass with a carbon-fibre brush before playing is usually enough.

How do I get rid of turntable hum?

Connect the turntable's ground wire to the GND terminal on the amplifier or phono preamp, and keep the turntable away from the amplifier and from power supplies.

How can I tell if the stylus is worn?

If the noise persists even on clean records and the sound has become muddy or distorted, have it checked: styli wear out and need replacing.

Why does my turntable sound distorted or too quiet?

It's almost always the phono preamp: you need exactly one. Check the turntable's PHONO/LINE switch and the amplifier input you're using.
