How to choose speaker stands Height, top plate, weight, material and what goes underneath: what to look at before you buy

A stand looks like nothing more than a post with a plate on top. But get it wrong and the speaker sounds muddier, or rocks every time you walk past. Below is what to look at, starting with what matters most.

By Fabrizio Berlati · Artisan workshop in Cesena

First of all, height.

It matters most, and it's also what people get wrong most often. The tweeter, the small treble driver, should be at the height of your ears when you're seated. So there's no single right height: it depends on how high your sofa is and where the tweeter sits on your speaker.

A low sofa calls for lower stands than you'd expect. Instead of guessing, use the speaker stand height calculator: enter two measurements and it tells you how tall the stand should be.

The top plate.

Grab a tape measure and measure the base of your speaker, width and depth. Then compare it with the stand's top plate. Ideally the two are about the same.

If the speaker overhangs by a couple of centimetres, nothing happens. If it overhangs a lot, it sits on too small a surface and one bump is enough to make it rock. The opposite is less of a problem: a small speaker on a big plate stays put, it just looks a bit lost.

Heavy or light?

Even manufacturers don't agree on this one. A heavy stand stays still and isn't moved by the speaker's vibrations. A light but very stiff one gets rid of them quickly. Both can work well if they're well built.

What's never fine is a stand that flexes. The test takes ten seconds: put the speaker on top and push it gently with one finger, at the level of the plate. If the whole thing sways back and forth, the stand isn't doing its job.

Many metal stands are hollow and can be filled with sand to add weight and stability. A solid wood stand already has the weight built in.

Wood, metal or plywood.

No material sounds better than the others across the board. Stiffness and the way the stand is built matter more.

Metal is the most common; it's often hollow and can be filled. Solid wood is sturdy and lasts a lifetime, but it costs more and is harder to find. Wrapped chipboard or MDF is cheap, but the wrap can get damaged over the years. Plywood, made of wood sheets glued in cross layers, is stable and doesn't warp.

And there's one thing no spec sheet tells you: the stand lives in your living room and you see it every day. It's fair to choose it because you like it, too.

What goes underneath, and what goes on top.

Underneath, it depends on the floor. On tiles and wooden floors, rubber feet or felt pads work well, and they protect the floor too. On carpet or thick rugs people often use spikes, which go through the fabric and rest on the real floor. If you use spikes on a wooden floor, put spike shoes under them or they'll mark it.

On top, between stand and speaker, a few small rubber feet are enough: they hold the speaker in place and don't scratch it. Don't screw the speaker to the stand.

With children or pets at home.

A tall stand with a heavy speaker on top does damage if it falls. A wide, heavy base makes it far more stable. If you have small children or a dog running around the house, pick the stand with the widest base you can find and try bumping it: it should stay where it is.

The speaker brand's stand, or a universal one?

Some manufacturers make stands for their own speakers, and often they voiced the speakers on exactly those stands. If they exist, they're a safe choice. A universal stand works too, as long as it ticks the boxes above: the right height, a top plate that matches the speaker, and no wobble.

Frequently asked questions.

How tall should a speaker stand be?

Tall enough to bring the tweeter to ear height when you're seated. It depends on your sofa and your speaker, so it's worth measuring: the speaker stand height calculator does the maths for you.

Can the stand's top plate be smaller than the speaker?

A little, yes: a couple of centimetres of overhang is no problem. If the speaker overhangs a lot, it sits badly and rocks as soon as you bump it.

Is a heavy or a light stand better?

Both work, as long as they're stiff. The stand mustn't flex: with the speaker on top, a small push shouldn't make it sway.

Do speaker stands need spikes?

It depends on the floor. On carpet and thick rugs, spikes help the stand rest on the real floor underneath. On wooden floors and tiles, rubber feet or felt pads are fine, or spikes with spike shoes.

Is a wooden or a metal stand better?

Neither sounds better across the board: stiffness and build matter more. Metal can often be filled with sand; solid wood is already heavy and lasts a long time.
